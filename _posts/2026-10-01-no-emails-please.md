You've probably seen this setup, or at least most of it. I've seen versions of it in enough places that I no longer think of it as anyone's mistake. The SIEM sends high-severity alerts to a shared security mailbox, and within a month the analysts have written inbox rules for it: authentication noise into one folder, scanner findings into another, and anything from the backup platform into a folder that gets opened when a restore fails. I don't blame them. I'd have written the same rules. Now add one more rule, written by someone who phished an admin's credentials, that moves any message mentioning the compromised host into Deleted Items. Open the rule list afterwards and try to spot the odd one out. I can't, and I doubt you can, because each of those rules is a suppression policy written by whoever held the credentials at the time, with no author, no expiry and no review.

MITRE ATT&CK has a name for that last rule, [Email Hiding Rules (T1564.008)](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1564/008/), and hiding security alerts is one of the listed reasons attackers do it. Exchange transport rules can even do it for the whole organisation. The bit that bothers me most is that the alerts themselves were fine. They fired on time and they reached the mailbox. The trouble started afterwards, in the part of the system that decides who sees an alert, when they see it, and whether anyone has actually picked it up.

That's the part I want to talk about. Deciding whether an alert should fire at all is a detection-engineering conversation for another day, and Google's [SRE guidance](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/) on actionable pages covers it better than I would. My position on the rest is simple. Alerts are for responding to. Email is asynchronous, and it can be forgotten. The usual complaints, that email alerting is slow and noisy, both follow from that, and I've made both of them myself more than once. An alert that lands in an inbox gives someone a message, but the condition behind it still has no owner. No record exists of who took it or when, and nothing moves unless a person happens to read the email. The alerting work still gets done, mind you. Somebody still suppresses, groups, routes and escalates; they just do it by hand, in the inbox, with whatever rules they can write. I've started calling the missing piece custody, and email can't hold it, because it will happily wait for a reader for as long as it takes, and an alert can't afford to wait like that.

Incident responders already build their process around this. [ISO/IEC 27035-1](https://www.iso.org/standard/78973.html) separates reporting an event from assessing it and deciding what to do, and FIRST's [CSIRT Services Framework](https://www.first.org/standards/frameworks/csirts/csirt_services_framework_v2.1) treats receiving a report and triaging it as two distinct jobs. Email gets you through the first one and leaves the rest to whoever happens to open the message.

## SMTP is patient by design

I sometimes have to remind people, and now and then myself, that SMTP was built to survive failure, and speed came second. [RFC 5321](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5321#section-4.5.4.1) tells a sender to queue mail it can't deliver, retry at intervals of generally at least 30 minutes, and keep at it for four to five days before giving up. For letters, that's exactly right. On a normal day mail shows up in seconds, so it goes unnoticed until a relay, a tenant or a filtering appliance has a bad hour. If your paging target is five minutes, you're leaning on a promise the protocol didn't make.

Delivery is also the wrong thing to measure in an incident. A delivered email tells me a mailbox accepted it. I want to know that a person accepted the work, and SMTP can't tell me that.

## This incident will be reported

{% include gallery id="galleryXkcd" caption="[xkcd 838: "Incident"](https://xkcd.com/838/) by Randall Munroe, licensed under [CC BY-NC 2.5](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/2.5/)." %}

sudo has been promising us for decades that "this incident will be reported". Randall Munroe asked who it reports to, and the [sudoers manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man5/sudoers.5.html) answers plainly. Mail goes to "the proper authorities", which by default means `root`, through whatever local mailer the box happens to have. The same manual says every attempt is logged whether or not mail is sent, and since version 1.9 sudo can ship those logs to a remote log server. Even sudo's own authors put the real record in the logs and treated the email as a courtesy.

sudo isn't alone, either. Open the man pages for the tools we lean on and the same design keeps turning up. [cron](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man5/crontab.5.html) mails a job's output to whoever owns the crontab unless `MAILTO` says otherwise, and the page even admits the UUCP account tends not to read its mail. [mdadm](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man5/mdadm.conf.5.html) takes exactly one `MAILADDR` for a degraded array and silently ignores any extra addresses, so one person hears about your RAID losing a disk. [auditd](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man5/auditd.conf.5.html) is the one I worry about most. Its own documentation recommends email as the first warning that audit storage is running out, sends it to root through `/usr/lib/sendmail`, and does it once. If nobody reads that message, the next thing to go is the audit trail itself.

Even [Wazuh](https://wazuh.com/?utm_source=ambassadors&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=ambassadors+program), the SIEM I'm an [ambassador](https://wazuh.com/ambassadors-program/?utm_source=ambassadors&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=ambassadors+program) of, does it. It can [mail alerts straight from the manager](https://documentation.wazuh.com/current/user-manual/manager/alert-management.html), but it doesn't handle SMTP authentication, so in practice you run a local relay next to it. By default it also sends at most twelve alert emails an hour and holds the rest for one combined message at the end of the hour. That's a sensible way to protect an inbox, and it also means an alert email can arrive up to an hour late by design.

## A distribution list is a team

Email routes alerts through distribution lists, and a distribution list is a team. At three in the morning the alert needs one person, whoever is on call. The list wakes, or rather fails to wake, eight people, and the seven who are asleep reasonably assume the eighth has it. [Darley and Latané](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025589) called this diffusion of responsibility back in 1968, and a distribution list is about the most efficient way I know to produce it. The standard advice for inbox overload is to trim each list down to the responsible team. It helps with aim, but you're still addressing a team, and nothing in the path knows who holds the pager tonight. So when the first person stays quiet, the system has no one to escalate to.

Grouping breaks for the same reason. One upstream outage can raise three hundred alerts, and SMTP dutifully delivers three hundred separate messages, because nothing along the way knows they share a cause. People cope by writing rules that file alerts into folders, and from then on an alert gets read when someone remembers the folder exists. Moving the same stream into a Teams channel or a mobile push doesn't fix either problem. The new channel still addresses a group and still sends one message per alert. My rough rule is to count noise at this layer as two numbers: how many notifications one condition produces, and how many people each one reaches. Swapping the channel leaves both where they were.

## The encryption debate misses the stored copy

Whenever someone says "email is insecure", someone else brings up [RFC 8314](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8314) and [MTA-STS](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8461), and I think they're both arguing about the wrong layer. Those protect mail in transit between servers. The objection I'd take seriously is about alert content sitting in plain text at the endpoints, and encrypting the hops does nothing for the copy in the mailbox.

To be fair to email, an alerting platform stores alerts too, so "it's stored" doesn't settle anything. I care about how many copies exist and who can read them. An email alert sits in the shared mailbox, then in journaling, archiving and e-discovery holds, then on the phones that sync the mailbox, and the delegates and forwarding rules on that mailbox can read it too. Security alerts carry hostnames, account names, indicators and sometimes whole command lines. Each copy widens the audience, and transport encryption doesn't touch any of them. That durability is why email works so well as an archive of what people said to each other, and it's the same reason email is the wrong home for content that names your hosts and accounts.

## Custody belongs somewhere that can hold it

Alerts still get suppressed after they leave the inbox. The difference is that someone signs the suppression. [Prometheus Alertmanager](https://prometheus.io/docs/alerting/latest/alertmanager/) groups related alerts, holds back downstream alerts while a root-cause alert is firing, and applies silences that carry a creator, a comment and an end time. A silence does the same job as an inbox rule, and I like it a lot better: the whole team can see it, it expires, and it tells you who created it. An on-call platform adds the lifecycle a mailbox can't hold. [PagerDuty's incident model](https://support.pagerduty.com/main/docs/incidents) is the familiar example, where an incident goes from triggered to acknowledged to resolved, the schedule resolves to whoever is on call, acknowledging makes you the owner, and if nobody acknowledges in time, it escalates. Google's SRE chapter on [managing incidents](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/) makes the same point from the people side, with one incident commander at a time and an explicit handoff whenever command changes hands.

I'd keep email out of the alert path entirely, including as an input to the alerting platform. Some platforms accept it, and it's tempting because nearly every product can send mail, but all that queueing and retrying applies on the way in just as much as on the way out. An alert stuck in a relay queue is late before anyone has had a chance to own it. If a product can only alert by email, I treat that as a gap in the product and push the vendor for a webhook or an API. Most of the tools above already have one: auditd can `exec` a script, mdadm has `PROGRAM`, sudo can ship to a log server, and Wazuh has [integrations](https://documentation.wazuh.com/current/user-manual/manager/integration-with-external-apis.html) that hand alerts to an on-call platform or a webhook.

Moving custody out of the inbox does bring one new chore that I find people forget, which is proving the alerting system itself is alive. A crashed alert manager sends nothing, and from the outside that looks the same as a quiet night. The usual answer is a heartbeat alert that fires all the time and pages someone through an independent path when it stops arriving. I wouldn't call a migration done without one.

Auditors notice the difference too. [PCI DSS v4.0.1](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/document_library/) Requirement 10.7.2 wants failures of critical security control systems detected, alerted and addressed promptly. Audit logging mechanisms are on its list, so that unread auditd email is in scope. Requirement 10.7.3 then asks you to document when each failure started and ended, and 12.10.5 sets the objective that alerts from security monitoring are responded to in a "structured, repeatable manner". A proper alerting system hands you those timestamps and that structure. An email path shows you a sent-items record, which proves a message left and not much else.

## Three questions for every alert path that ends in a mailbox

These are the questions I'd ask of each alert path that currently ends in a mailbox. Does anything other than a person reading the message record who owns the alert right now? Does each suppression on the path have an author, an expiry and a log entry the team can review? If the path itself broke, would anyone notice through a separate route? If the honest answer to any of these is "someone's inbox", that inbox is holding custody, and I'd fix that before spending a minute on which channel to use. I've yet to see a path I'd bet my own on-call night on without asking these first.

None of this is a complaint about email itself. Email is great for long-term archival and for people talking to people, and it's fine for reports, as long as they come weekly or less often. Anything more frequent goes unread the same way the alerts did, just a little more politely.

If you want somewhere to start on Monday, you don't need a new product. Go through the rules on each mailbox that receives alerts, and while you're at it, grep your `/etc` for `MAILTO`, `MAILADDR`, `mailto` and `action_mail_acct`. It's the least glamorous job in alerting and probably the most useful. Those rules and settings are already your alerting configuration, just without version control or review, and from the day you find them, I'd treat a change to any of them the same way as a change to an Alertmanager route.
